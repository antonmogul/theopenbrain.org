## Chapter 1: Foundations of Neuroscience (24 cards)

**1. Which four debates does this chapter use to trace the history of our understanding of the brain?**

- **A:** 1) Where is the mind? 2) Is there localization of function in the brain? 3) What is the fundamental functional unit of the brain? 4) How do neurons communicate with one another?
- **Section:** Introduction (paragraph 1)
- **Quote:** “1) Where is the mind? 2) Is there localization of function in the brain? 3) What is the fundamental functional unit of the brain? 4) How do neurons communicate with one another?”
- **Difficulty:** 1 (easy) · **Tags:** overview

**2. What did Paul Broca conclude about the Incan skull with a square hole that Squier sent him, and what did his report spark?**

- **A:** That the person had survived at least a few days after the procedure. His published findings sparked immediate interest in studying trepanation (surgically putting a hole in the skull) in ancient burial sites.
- **Section:** Where is my mind? (paragraph 0)
- **Quote:** “Broca was convinced that this was the skull of a person who had survived at least for a few days after the procedure. Broca published his findings, which generated immediate interest in studying trepanation—the surgical procedure of putting a hole in the skull—in ancient burial sites.”
- **Difficulty:** 2 (medium) · **Tags:** trepanation, broca

**3. What role did Hippocrates of Cos and his followers assign to the brain?**

- **A:** They considered it the seat of intelligence and consciousness, believed it controlled many bodily functions, and saw it as the root of what we now call neurological disorders and mental illnesses.
- **Section:** Where is my mind? (paragraph 6)
- **Quote:** “Hippocrates of Cos and his followers considered the brain to be the seat of intelligence and consciousness, believed the brain controlled many bodily functions, and thought the brain was at the root of what are now considered neurological disorders and mental illnesses”
- **Difficulty:** 1 (easy) · **Tags:** seat-of-mind, hippocrates

**4. How did Plato divide the tripartite soul across the body?**

- **A:** The head/brain held an immortal, rational soul; the chest held a mortal soul that was the source of our feelings; the gut held the soul of nourishment and passions.
- **Section:** Where is my mind? (paragraph 7)
- **Quote:** “Plato argued for a triune, or tripartite, soul: the head or brain contained an immortal, rational soul; the chest contained a mortal soul that was the source of our feelings; the gut region contained the soul of nourishment and passions.”
- **Difficulty:** 2 (medium) · **Tags:** seat-of-mind, plato

**5. Why did Aristotle hold a cardiocentric view, and what function did he give the brain?**

- **A:** He considered the heart warm and therefore vital, and thought the brain was cold; he argued the brain served to cool down the heart. (Galen later tested this and found the brain was warm.)
- **Section:** Where is my mind? (paragraph 8)
- **Quote:** “he argued for a cardiocentric view because he considered the heart to be warm, and thus vital, whereas he thought the brain was cold—Galen would test this a few hundred years later and find that the brain was warm. Aristotle argued that the brain served to cool down the heart”
- **Difficulty:** 2 (medium) · **Tags:** seat-of-mind, aristotle

**6. By dissecting human bodies in Alexandria, which brain structures did Herophilus and Erasistratus describe for the first time?**

- **A:** The cerebrum, the cerebellum and the cerebral ventricles of the human brain. Dissecting executed criminals, they pioneered the field of human anatomy.
- **Section:** Where is my mind? (paragraph 9)
- **Quote:** “By dissecting bodies of criminals executed at the Ptolemean court, these Greek physicians pioneered the field of human anatomy and provided the first descriptions of the cerebrum, the cerebellum, and the cerebral ventricles in the human brain.”
- **Difficulty:** 2 (medium) · **Tags:** anatomy, dissection

**7. In Flourens's lesion experiments, what did removing the cerebrum versus the cerebellum appear to impair?**

- **A:** Removing the cerebrum appeared to impair sensory perception and judgement; removing the cerebellum seemed to impair equilibrium and coordination.
- **Section:** Where is my mind? (paragraph 11)
- **Quote:** “He found that removing the cerebrum appeared to impair sensory perception and judgement, while removing the cerebellum seemed to impair equilibrium and coordination.”
- **Difficulty:** 2 (medium) · **Tags:** lesions, flourens

**8. In the cell doctrine (ventricular theory), who first assigned Galen's three mental faculties to brain locations, and where?**

- **A:** Christian Church Fathers of the 4th century CE took Galen's three mental faculties (imagination, reasoning and memory) and assigned each to a different ventricle of the brain.
- **Section:** Do different parts of the brain do different things? (paragraph 2)
- **Quote:** “components—imagination, reasoning, and memory—he did not propose specific brain locations where these were processed. That task would be taken up by Christian Church Fathers of the 4th century CE. They took these faculties and assigned each to a different ventricle in the brain.”
- **Difficulty:** 2 (medium) · **Tags:** localization, ventricles

**9. What role did Descartes give the pineal gland?**

- **A:** He argued it acted like a puppet master, directing the flow of spirits through the brain to control distinct functions and acting as the interface between the physical and ethereal worlds.
- **Section:** Do different parts of the brain do different things? (paragraph 4)
- **Quote:** “Descartes argued that the pineal gland, which lies along the midline, acted like a puppet master, directing the flow of spirits throughout the brain to control distinct functions, and acting as an interface between the physical and ethereal worlds.”
- **Difficulty:** 1 (easy) · **Tags:** dualism, descartes

**10. What were Franz Joseph Gall's three core beliefs about the brain and individual disposition?**

- **A:** Different parts of the brain serve different functions; the relative size of each functional area differs across individuals; and these anatomical differences account for differences in disposition.
- **Section:** Do different parts of the brain do different things? (paragraph 10)
- **Quote:** “believed that different parts of the brain served different functions, that the relative size of each of these functional areas differed across individuals, and that these anatomical differences accounted for differences in disposition.”
- **Difficulty:** 2 (medium) · **Tags:** phrenology, localization

**11. On what grounds did Flourens reject Gall's phrenology?**

- **A:** In contrast to Gall, he considered the cerebrum to be equipotential: all sensations, all perceptions and all volition occupy the same seat in it.
- **Section:** Do different parts of the brain do different things? (paragraph 12)
- **Quote:** “However, in contrast to Gall, Flourens considered the cerebrum to be equipotential: “All sensations, all perceptions, and all volition occupy concurrently the same seat in these organs.”
- **Difficulty:** 2 (medium) · **Tags:** phrenology, equipotentiality

**12. From the patient Leborgne ('Tan') and later patients with aphasia, which region did Broca identify as the locus of speech generation?**

- **A:** The left frontal cortex; a follow-up paper with several additional people with aphasia solidified this claim.
- **Section:** Do different parts of the brain do different things? (paragraph 14)
- **Quote:** “a few years later published a follow-up paper with several additional people with aphasia, solidifying his claim of the left frontal cortex as the locus of speech generation”
- **Difficulty:** 1 (easy) · **Tags:** localization, speech, broca

**13. What did Fritsch and Hitzig find when they electrically stimulated the cortex of dogs?**

- **A:** A specific portion of cortex (motor cortex) reliably drove contralateral body movements, and stimulating different parts of it moved different parts of the body.
- **Section:** Do different parts of the brain do different things? (paragraph 15)
- **Quote:** “Not only did they find a specific portion of cortex that could reliably drive contralateral body movements, they found that stimulating different parts of this motor cortex led to movement of different parts of the body”
- **Difficulty:** 2 (medium) · **Tags:** motor-cortex, localization

**14. What did Karl Lashley conclude from lesioning rat brains in his search for engrams?**

- **A:** That memories were stored everywhere (equipotentiality) and that the impact of a lesion scaled directly with its size (mass action).
- **Section:** Do different parts of the brain do different things? (paragraph 17)
- **Quote:** “This led him to conclude that unlike primary sensory cortical regions which took up defined plots of land in the brain, memories were stored everywhere (equipotentiality) and that the impact of lesions directly scaled with the size of the lesion (mass action).”
- **Difficulty:** 2 (medium) · **Tags:** memory, equipotentiality

**15. What two conclusions did Brenda Milner draw from studying patient H.M. (Henry Molaison)?**

- **A:** The hippocampus is critical for encoding new memories, and different types of memory (episodic vs. procedural) are stored in different brain regions.
- **Section:** Do different parts of the brain do different things? (paragraph 18)
- **Quote:** “not only was Milner able to identify the hippocampus as a critical region for the encoding of new memories, she was also able to establish that different types of memories—e.g., episodic (“I remember what happened where”) vs. procedural (“I know how to do it”)—were stored in different brain regions.”
- **Difficulty:** 2 (medium) · **Tags:** memory, hippocampus

**16. What did the reticular theory, formalized by Joseph von Gerlach in 1871, propose about how the brain is built?**

- **A:** That the brain is built from specialized cells whose extended fibers fuse with one another, forming a giant nerve net or syncytium.
- **Section:** What's the basic functional unit of the brain? (paragraph 4)
- **Quote:** “he proposed that the brain is built from specialized cells with extended fibers that fused with one another, forming a giant nerve net or syncytium”
- **Difficulty:** 2 (medium) · **Tags:** reticular-theory, neuron-doctrine

**17. What was the main advantage of Camillo Golgi's 'black reaction' silver stain?**

- **A:** Random cells in nervous tissue are stained very darkly and in their entirety.
- **Section:** What's the basic functional unit of the brain? (paragraph 5)
- **Quote:** “The main advantage of this staining method, which is still used by some researchers today, is that random cells in nervous tissue are very darkly stained in their entirety.”
- **Difficulty:** 1 (easy) · **Tags:** histology, golgi

**18. What did Ramón y Cajal conclude about how neuronal processes meet other cells, and what was this contact later called?**

- **A:** Neuronal processes end in close apposition to other cell bodies or processes but never fuse with them. Charles Sherrington later (1897) named this close apposition a synapse.
- **Section:** What's the basic functional unit of the brain? (paragraph 7)
- **Quote:** “he concluded that such processes terminate in close apposition to other structures (cell bodies or processes) but never fuse with them. This close apposition was later called a synapse (from the Greek “to clasp”) by Charles Sherrington in 1897.”
- **Difficulty:** 2 (medium) · **Tags:** neuron-doctrine, synapse, cajal

**19. What does Ramón y Cajal's law of dynamic polarization describe?**

- **A:** The direction of information flow within a neuron: from dendrites to cell body to axon.
- **Section:** What's the basic functional unit of the brain? (paragraph 9)
- **Quote:** “his remarkable power of deduction allowed him to identify the flow of information within cells (the law of dynamic polarization; from dendrites to cell body to axon)”
- **Difficulty:** 1 (easy) · **Tags:** neuron-doctrine, cajal

**20. What were the two sides of the 'Soups vs. Sparks' debate?**

- **A:** 'Soups': chemical components must mediate neurotransmission. 'Sparks': electricity was the only medium that could reasonably carry fast messages between neurons.
- **Section:** How do neurons communicate? (paragraph 2)
- **Quote:** “with some being convinced that chemical components (soups) must mediate neurotransmission, while others firmly believed that electricity (sparks) was the only medium that could reasonably convey fast messages between neurons”
- **Difficulty:** 1 (easy) · **Tags:** neurotransmission

**21. In Loewi's frog-heart experiment, what did fluid from the vagus-stimulated heart do to a second heart, and what did this show?**

- **A:** The second (denervated) heart began beating more slowly, showing that the stimulated vagus nerve releases a molecule that affects heart rate.
- **Section:** How do neurons communicate? (paragraph 5)
- **Quote:** “Then, he collected the solution in which the heart was bathed and applied it to a second (denervated) heart, which began beating more slowly when exposed to the solution. This demonstrated that the vagus nerve, when stimulated, led to release of a molecule that impacted heart rate.”
- **Difficulty:** 2 (medium) · **Tags:** neurotransmission, loewi

**22. What did Henry Dale and colleagues show Loewi's 'Vagusstoff' to be?**

- **A:** Acetylcholine, which made it the first neurotransmitter to be identified.
- **Section:** How do neurons communicate? (paragraph 6)
- **Quote:** “Henry Dale, who had trained with Langley at Cambridge, demonstrated with his colleagues that Vagusstoff was in fact acetylcholine, making it the first neurotransmitter to be identified”
- **Difficulty:** 1 (easy) · **Tags:** neurotransmission, acetylcholine

**23. In Eccles's spinal-cord experiment, what result would indicate chemical inhibition, and what did he actually record?**

- **A:** If the interneuron released an inhibitory neurotransmitter, the motor neuron would hyperpolarize. To his surprise, Eccles recorded a hyperpolarization.
- **Section:** How do neurons communicate? (paragraph 8)
- **Quote:** “If, on the other hand, interneuron G released an inhibitory neurotransmitter, a hyperpolarization would be detected in the motor neuron. To his great surprise, Eccles recorded a hyperpolarization.”
- **Difficulty:** 3 (hard) · **Tags:** neurotransmission, inhibition, eccles

**24. How do electrical synapses arise, what role do they often play, and what does this mean for 'soups vs. sparks'?**

- **A:** They arise via gap junctions and often synchronize neuronal activity. So soups and sparks work together in the brain.
- **Section:** How do neurons communicate? (paragraph 9)
- **Quote:** “roles have been established for electrical synapses, which arise via gap junctions, often involving synchronizing neuronal activity. Thus, soups and sparks work together in the brain.”
- **Difficulty:** 2 (medium) · **Tags:** electrical-synapses, gap-junctions

## Chapter 2: The Retina (24 cards)

**1. What is the difference between the intromission and extramission theories of vision?**

- **A:** Intromission: vision works by light passing into the eyes. Extramission: vision works by energy passing out of the eyes. Many of the earliest theories favoured extramission.
- **Section:** The Retina (paragraph 0)
- **Quote:** “does vision work via intromission (eg. light passing into the eyes) or extramission (eg. energy passing out of the eyes). Many of the earliest theories favored the extramission model of vision.”
- **Difficulty:** 1 (easy) · **Tags:** history, vision-theories

**2. Which provides more refractive power to the eye, the cornea or the lens, and how can you notice this?**

- **A:** The cornea. You can see this if you open your eyes under water, because water and the cornea have similar refractive indices.
- **Section:** Story of the eye (EDIT) (paragraph 1)
- **Quote:** “The cornea provides more refractive power to the eye than the lens, and this can be seen if you open your eyes under water, as water and the cornea have similar refractive indices.”
- **Difficulty:** 2 (medium) · **Tags:** optics, cornea

**3. What are the five neuronal classes of the retina?**

- **A:** Photoreceptors, horizontal cells, bipolar cells, amacrine cells and ganglion cells.
- **Section:** Organization and cell types in the retina (paragraph 2)
- **Quote:** “The retina is comprised of 5 neuronal classes – photoreceptors, horizontal cells, bipolar cells amacrine cells, and ganglion cells”
- **Difficulty:** 1 (easy) · **Tags:** cell-classes

**4. Why does each eye have a small blind spot?**

- **A:** Ganglion cell axons bundle into the optic nerve, which passes back through the retina to leave the eye; the optic nerve head contains no photoreceptors.
- **Section:** Organization and cell types in the retina (paragraph 2)
- **Quote:** “Axons from all ganglion cells get bundled together into the optic nerve, which ‘dives’ back through the entire retina before leaving the eye, resulting in a small blind spot in each eye’s field of view (as the location of the optic nerve head contains no photoreceptors).”
- **Difficulty:** 1 (easy) · **Tags:** anatomy, blind-spot

**5. What is a retinal 'mosaic'?**

- **A:** A pattern of laterally tiled cells: each ganglion cell and its upstream microcircuitry is replicated across the retinal surface, so visual information is extracted from every part of the visual field.
- **Section:** Organization and cell types in the retina (paragraph 6)
- **Quote:** “Each ganglion cell (and its upstream microcircuitry) are replicated laterally over the entire retinal surface, such that visual information is extracted from each part of the visual field. Such patterns of laterally tiled cells are referred to as mosaics.”
- **Difficulty:** 2 (medium) · **Tags:** mosaics, lateral-organization

**6. Which neurotransmitters do almost all amacrine cells release, and how does transmitter type relate to dendritic tree size?**

- **A:** GABA or glycine (they are inhibitory). Glycinergic amacrine cells tend to have narrow dendritic trees and small receptive fields; GABAergic ones tend to have broader trees and larger receptive fields.
- **Section:** Organization and cell types in the retina (paragraph 13)
- **Quote:** “almost all amacrine cells are inhibitory neurons releasing either GABA or glycine onto their targets; Second, glycinergic amacrine cells tend to have narrow dendritic trees, and thus small receptive fields, whereas GABAergic cells tend to have broader dendritic trees and thus larger receptive fields”
- **Difficulty:** 2 (medium) · **Tags:** amacrine-cells

**7. How many cone types do humans have, and roughly where do their peak sensitivities lie?**

- **A:** Three: blue (~420 nm), green (~534 nm) and red (~564 nm).
- **Section:** Photoreceptors and phototransduction (paragraph 0)
- **Quote:** “Humans have 3 cone types, with maximal photon absorptions situated in the blue (peak excitation ~420 nm), green (peak excitation ~534 nm) and red (peak excitation ~564 nm) regions of the electromagnetic spectrum.”
- **Difficulty:** 1 (easy) · **Tags:** photoreceptors, cones

**8. What shape are rod outer segments, what light conditions are rods specialized for, and which pigment do their discs contain?**

- **A:** Long, rod-shaped outer segments; specialized for detecting photons in dim environments; their discs contain rhodopsin, tuned to blue-green light (peak ~498 nm).
- **Section:** Photoreceptors and phototransduction (paragraph 0)
- **Quote:** “In contrast to cones, rods have long (rod-shaped) outer segments, and are specialized for detecting photons in dim environments. Discs in rod outer segments contain rhodopsin molecules attuned to wavelengths at the center of the visible range (blue-green; peak excitation ~498 nm).”
- **Difficulty:** 1 (easy) · **Tags:** photoreceptors, rods

**9. What molecular event underlies rhodopsin 'bleaching', and what does it trigger?**

- **A:** Retinal photoisomerizes from the 11-cis to the all-trans form. This changes rhodopsin's conformation, which starts a signal transduction cascade.
- **Section:** Photoreceptors and phototransduction (paragraph 4)
- **Quote:** “The bleaching that Boll had observed was the result of retinal undergoing a photoisomerization from 11-cis to all-trans conformations. This isomerization causes rhodopsin to change conformation which initiates a signal transduction cascade.”
- **Difficulty:** 2 (medium) · **Tags:** phototransduction, rhodopsin

**10. Why does spreading thermal noise across many tiny-current cGMP channels help photoreceptors detect light?**

- **A:** Each channel's noise is independent, so it averages out, while the photon signal is synchronized across channels and photoreceptors, so it averages in.
- **Section:** Photoreceptors and phototransduction (paragraph 7)
- **Quote:** “The noise of different channels is independent, and thus is averaged out. In contrast, the signal (i.e. the detection of photons produced by a flash of light) is synchronized across channels and photoreceptors, and is averaged in.”
- **Difficulty:** 3 (hard) · **Tags:** phototransduction, signal-and-noise

**11. Where in the visual pathway does center-surround antagonism begin, and which cells implement it there?**

- **A:** At the first synapse of the visual pathway, where it is implemented by horizontal cells.
- **Section:** Horizontal cells and feedback (paragraph 0)
- **Quote:** “Center-surround antagonism begins at the first synapse of the visual pathway, where it is implemented by horizontal cells.”
- **Difficulty:** 1 (easy) · **Tags:** horizontal-cells, center-surround

**12. Why does a star in the night sky evoke little horizontal cell feedback, while a cloudless blue sky evokes a lot?**

- **A:** A small stimulus like a star activates only a few cones, whereas a large stimulus like a blue sky activates many adjacent cones and evokes significant horizontal cell feedback.
- **Section:** Horizontal cells and feedback (paragraph 1)
- **Quote:** “a small visual stimulus that only activates a small number of cones – such as a star in the sky – evokes little horizontal cell feedback. In contrast, large visual stimuli that activate many adjacent cones – such as a cloudless blue sky – evoke significant horizontal cell feedback.”
- **Difficulty:** 2 (medium) · **Tags:** horizontal-cells, feedback

**13. How does an increase in light depolarize ON bipolar cells?**

- **A:** Light lowers glutamate in the synaptic cleft, which releases mGluR6's inhibition of the TRPM1 channel; this drives an inward current and depolarizes the ON bipolar cell.
- **Section:** Bipolar cells diversify photoreceptor signals (paragraph 2)
- **Quote:** “Increments in light intensity lower glutamate levels in the synaptic cleft, which releases the inhibition of TRPM1 by mGluR6, driving an inward current and depolarization of ON bipolar cells.”
- **Difficulty:** 3 (hard) · **Tags:** bipolar-cells, on-off

**14. Why do bipolar cells signal mean luminance rather than the spatial pattern of a stimulus?**

- **A:** Their responses are linear at this stage of the circuit, so they signal the mean luminance over all connected photoreceptors, not the stimulus's spatial organization.
- **Section:** Bipolar cells diversify photoreceptor signals (paragraph 3)
- **Quote:** “Linear responses at this level of the retinal circuit mean that bipolar cells signal the mean luminance over all their connected photoreceptors, rather than encoding the spatial organization of the incoming stimulus.”
- **Difficulty:** 3 (hard) · **Tags:** bipolar-cells, linearity

**15. Which inputs drive the center and the surround of a ganglion cell's receptive field?**

- **A:** Center: excitatory bipolar cell input collected over the ganglion cell's dendritic tree. Surround: mainly GABAergic amacrine cells located laterally further away.
- **Section:** Amacrine and ganglion cells – circuits, computations and output (paragraph 3)
- **Quote:** “Center responses are driven by excitatory bipolar cell input which is collected over a ganglion cell’s dendritic tree. Surround responses are driven predominantly by GABAergic amacrine cells that reside (laterally) further away and relay visual signals to the central ganglion cell.”
- **Difficulty:** 2 (medium) · **Tags:** center-surround, ganglion-cells

**16. Which ganglion cells are thought to compute red-green and blue-yellow color opponency in the primate retina?**

- **A:** Red-green: midget ganglion cells (some sample mainly red or green cones in their centers). Blue-yellow: small bistratified ganglion cells.
- **Section:** Amacrine and ganglion cells – circuits, computations and output (paragraph 5)
- **Quote:** “Red-green opponency is thought to be computed in midget ganglion cells, some of which exhibit a biased spectral sampling within their receptive field centers to either red or green cones. In contrast, blue-yellow opponency is thought to be computed by small bistratified ganglion cells”
- **Difficulty:** 3 (hard) · **Tags:** color-opponency, ganglion-cells

**17. Why does null-direction motion evoke little response in a direction-selective ganglion cell, despite symmetrical bipolar excitation?**

- **A:** Null-direction motion drives strong GABAergic inhibition that vetoes the bipolar excitation; preferred-direction motion drives very little inhibition.
- **Section:** Amacrine and ganglion cells – circuits, computations and output (paragraph 7)
- **Quote:** “stimuli moving in a ganglion cell’s preferred direction drive very little inhibition, whereas stimuli moving in the ganglion cell’s null direction drive strong GABAergic inhibition, which vetoes the relatively symmetrical excitatory drive from bipolar cells”
- **Difficulty:** 3 (hard) · **Tags:** direction-selectivity, starburst-amacrine

**18. Rod bipolar cells do not synapse onto ganglion cells. How do rod signals reach the ON and OFF pathways?**

- **A:** Rod bipolar cells make glutamatergic synapses onto AII amacrine cells, which pass rod signals to ON bipolar cells via electrical synapses and to OFF bipolar cells via glycinergic synapses.
- **Section:** Amacrine and ganglion cells – circuits, computations and output (paragraph 12)
- **Quote:** “Instead, rod bipolar cells make glutamatergic synapses with AII (pronounced A-2) amacrine cells. AII amacrine cells pass rod signals to ON bipolars via electrical synapses, and OFF bipolars via glycinergic synapses.”
- **Difficulty:** 3 (hard) · **Tags:** rod-pathway, amacrine-cells

**19. What makes intrinsically photosensitive retinal ganglion cells (ipRGCs) special, and what do their inputs control?**

- **A:** They contain melanopsin and turn light into spikes without rod or cone input. They project to 'non-image forming' centers, where they help control circadian rhythm and the pupillary light reflex.
- **Section:** Amacrine and ganglion cells – circuits, computations and output (paragraph 14)
- **Quote:** “these cells contain melanopsin and can convert light into spiking responses in the absence of inputs from rod and cone circuits. ipRGCs project to several ‘non-image forming’ visual centers in the brain, where their inputs are important for controlling circadian rhythm and the pupillary light reflex”
- **Difficulty:** 2 (medium) · **Tags:** iprgcs, melanopsin

**20. How does the retina let us see over roughly 10 orders of magnitude of light intensity?**

- **A:** The retina as a whole adapts to the mean luminance of the environment, so a ganglion cell's luminance response curve shifts with mean luminance.
- **Section:** Retinal vision in the ‘real world’ (paragraph 4)
- **Quote:** “the retina as a whole is able to adapt to the mean luminance of a given environment, meaning that the luminance response curve of a ganglion cells can shift depending on mean luminance, allowing us to see over roughly 10 orders of magnitude”
- **Difficulty:** 2 (medium) · **Tags:** light-adaptation

**21. Why are spontaneous retinal waves in the developing retina important for the brain?**

- **A:** They spread to retino-recipient areas of the brain, where the patterned activity is important for developing retinotopic maps; disrupting the waves impairs proper map development.
- **Section:** Retinal development (paragraph 5)
- **Quote:** “These waves of retinal activity propagate to retino-recipient areas in the brain, where this patterned activity is important for the development of retinotopic maps. Pharmacologically or genetically disrupting wave activity has been shown to inhibit the development of proper retinotopic maps”
- **Difficulty:** 2 (medium) · **Tags:** development, retinal-waves

**22. In age-related macular degeneration (AMD), which cells are damaged and what visual deficit results?**

- **A:** Photoreceptors in the macula, specifically cones (dominant there), leading to a blind spot (scotoma) in the high-acuity central visual field.
- **Section:** Diseases, disorders, and treatments (paragraph 8)
- **Quote:** “This results in damage to photoreceptors (specifically cones which are dominant in the macula), leading to a blind spot (scotoma) in the high acuity central part of the visual field.”
- **Difficulty:** 2 (medium) · **Tags:** disease, amd

**23. Why does retinitis pigmentosa lead to 'tunnel vision' and night-blindness?**

- **A:** RP is a group of genetic diseases causing loss of rod photoreceptors, and rods dominate the peripheral retina (outside the fovea/macula).
- **Section:** Diseases, disorders, and treatments (paragraph 10)
- **Quote:** “Retinitis pigmentosa (RP) is a group of diverse genetic diseases that lead to loss of rod photoreceptors. Since rods are dominant in the peripheral retina (outside the fovea/macula), retinitis pigmentosa leads to ‘tunnel vision’ and night-blindness.”
- **Difficulty:** 2 (medium) · **Tags:** disease, retinitis-pigmentosa

**24. Why does the chapter call for more retinal research in foveated animal models rather than mice?**

- **A:** Mice lack a fovea, a crucial part of the human visual system. Foveated models are needed to examine differences in cell types and circuit computations between foveal and peripheral retina.
- **Section:** Looking forward (paragraph 2)
- **Quote:** “mice lack a fovea, which is a crucial part of the human visual system. In the coming years, increased studies in foveated animal models will be required to examine difference in cell types and circuit computations in foveal vs. peripheral retina”
- **Difficulty:** 2 (medium) · **Tags:** future-research, fovea
